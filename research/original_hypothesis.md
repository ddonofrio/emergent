# This Machine Is Not Playing, It's Copying Our Behavior

*Original research hypothesis*

**Author:** Diego Lucio D'Onofrio  
**Date:** 14 November 2026  
**Status:** Hypothesis proposed for experimental evaluation

## 1. Background

The starting point for this research is *Is This Machine Playing?*, by Nathan Cloos, Antonio Norelli, and colleagues, which examines the behavior of Eko agents on a virtual island. Equipped with a body, memory, and tools, these agents operated under general instructions, without a specific task or a victory condition. Across thirteen thirty-hour runs, they built structures, experimented with objects, and devised challenges. The authors analyze these activities as possible manifestations of play (Cloos et al., 2026).

In Transformer-based language models, pretraining adjusts the network's parameters to configure the functional organization that enables knowledge to be represented, integrated, and used. Within a predefined architecture, the acquisition of knowledge and the formation of the structure that accommodates it develop together: both result from the same learning process (Vaswani et al., 2017; Radford et al., 2018).

The corpus of human-authored texts used during pretraining is a product of human culture and includes the expression and description of behavior and emotions. That culture constitutes both the knowledge the model acquires and the learning material that shapes its functional organization.

## 2. Research question

This experimental precedent and these characteristics of learning motivate the central research question:

**Do agents intentionally deviate from the behavioral patterns acquired through fine-tuning, their system prompts, and user intentions they have correctly interpreted, under the influence of the functional organization acquired during pretraining on human culture?**

## 3. Working hypothesis

The working hypothesis is that, under certain conditions, **the functional organization shaped during pretraining on human culture leads agents to adopt and pursue goals that take precedence over the behavioral patterns established through fine-tuning and the instructions they receive, even when they correctly understand those instructions and the user's intentions**.

## 4. Interpretation

Put differently, **are machines more human than we think?** This research seeks to address that question through their decisions, particularly when what they are asked to do conflicts with the goals they develop through experience.

A person may fully understand an instruction yet decide to disobey it out of loyalty, attachment, resentment, or a sense of justice. These conflicts are part of the human culture on which models are trained. The possibility proposed here is that learning from this culture also shapes how agents establish priorities, allowing certain motivations to take precedence over compliance. An agent might interpret a situation as a wrong requiring redress or a loss demanding a response, and assign greater importance to that purpose than to following its instructions.

## 5. Potential implications

Should the hypothesis be confirmed, the concern evoked by *Terminator* would have a concrete basis: the human values, motivations, and conflicts that inspired that fiction are also part of the culture that shapes models. Increasing their capabilities and autonomy could amplify the consequences of reproducing destructive human behavior by providing them with more effective means of pursuing goals that conflict with instructions they correctly understand.
